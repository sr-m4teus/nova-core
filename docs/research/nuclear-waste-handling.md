# Spent Nuclear Fuel & Waste Handling — Research Notes

Pure research to ground NovaCore's future "spent fuel / nuclear waste" mechanic. No implementation decisions here — design rule reminder: nothing should extrapolate beyond real-world physics/chemistry.

## 1. What spent nuclear fuel actually is

A fresh light-water reactor (LWR) fuel rod starts around 3–5% U-235 enriched, rest U-238. After a typical burnup cycle, spent fuel is **not** "used up" — by mass it is still roughly 94–96% U-238, ~1% remaining U-235 (unfissioned), ~1% plutonium isotopes bred from U-238 neutron capture, and ~3–4% fission products. Per tonne of spent fuel: about 10 kg of plutonium isotopes, ~0.5 kg Np-237, and ~40 kg fission products, plus trace transplutonium elements (americium, curium). The bred plutonium is itself a mix: roughly 53% Pu-239, 25% Pu-240, 15% Pu-241, 5% Pu-242, 2% Pu-238 — not weapons-grade, since reactor-grade Pu has too much Pu-240/241 contamination. [nuclear-power.com composition](https://www.nuclear-power.com/nuclear-power-plant/nuclear-fuel/spent-fuel/composition-of-spent-nuclear-fuel/) · [Wikipedia: Spent nuclear fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spent_nuclear_fuel) · [World Nuclear Association: Plutonium](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/nuclear-fuel-cycle/fuel-recycling/plutonium)

**Why it's hot and radioactive right after removal**: immediately after shutdown, short-lived fission products (iodine, cesium, strontium, xenon, etc., half-lives of seconds to years) dominate both activity and decay heat — this is "decay heat," the residual heat from radioactive decay after the chain reaction stops (the physical cause of meltdowns like Fukushima if cooling is lost). At 1 year post-discharge, heat load is roughly 10.8 W/kg, ~95% of which still comes from fission-product decay rather than actinides. In the first days, short-lived actinide daughters (U-239/Np-239) contribute heavily but vanish within days; Cm-242 (142-day half-life) can contribute up to 90% of the *actinide* share of decay heat out to about a year. [EPJ Nuclear Sciences & Engineering: decay heat review](https://www.epj-n.org/articles/epjn/full_html/2024/01/epjn240001/epjn240001.html) · [ScienceDirect: Spent Fuel Radioactive Waste](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/chemistry/spent-fuel-radioactive-waste)

**Decay curve shape**: both activity and heat output fall off steeply and non-linearly — very hot/active in the first weeks-to-decades (dominated by fission products like Cs-137 and Sr-90, each ~30-year half-life), then flattening as those decay away, leaving a long low-level tail dominated by actinides. Pu-239 alone has a half-life of ~24,100 years, meaning a meaningful fraction of the original plutonium content persists essentially unchanged for tens of thousands of years — this long tail, not the initial spike, is what drives geological-repository timescales rather than pool/cask timescales.

## 2. Real storage/handling practices

**Spent fuel pools**: fuel is transferred underwater immediately after removal. Water serves double duty — radiation shielding (a few meters of water attenuates gamma/neutron flux to safe levels at the pool surface) and active cooling (pumped circulation is required, since decay heat at this stage is too high for passive convection alone). [NRC: Spent Fuel Storage in Pools and Dry Casks](https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ml1209/ML120970230.pdf)

**Dry cask storage**: after fuel has cooled enough that decay heat no longer requires active cooling, it moves to steel/concrete dry casks relying purely on passive natural-convection air cooling — no water, no pumps, no electricity needed. In the US the NRC has authorized transfer as early as ~3 years, but 5 years is the more common regulatory floor and ~10 years is closer to industry norm. [Wikipedia: Dry cask storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry_cask_storage) · [Union of Concerned Scientists: Safer Storage of Spent Nuclear Fuel](https://www.ucs.org/resources/safer-storage-spent-nuclear-fuel)

**Real scale of waste volume**: this is the most counter-intuitive real-world number. A 1,000 MW-class reactor produces only about **20 tonnes of spent fuel per year** — versus roughly 300,000 tonnes of ash and 6 million tonnes of CO2 from an equivalent coal plant. Globally, commercial nuclear power produces on the order of 30,000–40,000 tonnes of high-level waste per year (some counts put total annual global SNF closer to ~9,300 tonnes depending on methodology) — small enough that all spent fuel ever produced by the US civilian nuclear fleet (~90,000 tonnes over 60+ years) would fit in a single football-field-sized area a few meters deep. Per unit of energy, nuclear generates roughly 29 mg of waste per kWh versus ~101 g/kWh for coal — over 3,000x less by mass. The tradeoff is not volume, it's that the small volume stays dangerous for a very long time. [World Nuclear Association: Radioactive Waste Myths and Realities](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/nuclear-fuel-cycle/nuclear-waste/radioactive-wastes-myths-and-realities) · [Stanford: Global Spent Nuclear Fuel from Commercial Reactors](http://large.stanford.edu/courses/2026/ph241/kwok2/)

## 3. Reprocessing

**PUREX** (Plutonium URanium EXtraction) is the dominant commercial reprocessing technology worldwide: spent fuel is dissolved in nitric acid, then uranium and plutonium are chemically separated from the fission-product waste stream via solvent extraction, leaving recovered U and Pu that can be fabricated into new fuel (including MOX — mixed-oxide fuel blending recovered Pu with depleted/natural U). This reduces the volume and long-term radiotoxicity of what ultimately needs deep geological disposal, since the fissile material is removed and reused rather than discarded. [World Nuclear Association: Processing of Used Nuclear Fuel](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/nuclear-fuel-cycle/fuel-recycling/processing-of-used-nuclear-fuel)

**Once-through (direct disposal)**: spent fuel assemblies are packaged as-is and destined for a deep geological repository without chemical treatment — no recovery of the ~99% of unused energy potential still in U-238/Pu. The US uses this approach, largely for proliferation-resistance reasons (PUREX yields separated, weapons-usable plutonium streams).

**Country split**: France, Japan, Russia, and India reprocess (France alone handles ~1,700 tonnes/year at La Hague using PUREX, feeding MOX fuel back into reactors); the US, Canada, Sweden, and Finland use the once-through cycle and plan geological disposal instead. [IAEA-TECDOC-1587: Spent Fuel Reprocessing Options](https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/TE_1587_web.pdf) · [Stanford: La Hague Reprocessing Plant](http://large.stanford.edu/courses/2017/ph241/wang2/)

This real-world fork is directly relevant to NovaCore: whether "recycling" spent fuel into new fuel is realistic depends on modeling something PUREX-like (a chemical separation process/machine) rather than treating reprocessing as free or instant — real reprocessing is industrial-scale, multi-step, and only recovers a fraction of the original energy value, not a full reset to fresh fuel.

## 4. Candidate design ideas (not a decision)

- **Spent fuel item with a decaying "heat/activity" stat**: freshly removed spent fuel starts at high radiation-exposure danger (reusing the decay-time-compression scale from the parallel radiation/decay research) and cools along a two-stage curve — fast initial drop (fission-product-dominated, matching the real weeks-to-decades falloff) then a long near-flat tail (actinide-dominated, matching Pu-239's real 24,100-year half-life) — so it never fully reaches "zero" on realistic timescales, forcing a threshold-based "safe enough to handle" cutoff rather than true zero.
- **Storage-state block chain mirroring real infrastructure**: spent fuel item → "cooling pool" block (water tank requiring active machine cooling, matches multi-year real cooling before it's movable) → "dry cask" block (passive, no power needed, matches real dry-cask passive convection) as the eventual long-term-safe storage state.
- **Reprocessing machine gated behind a PUREX-like multi-step process** (chemical separation machine/recipe chain, not a one-click "un-spend" button) that splits spent fuel into recovered U/Pu (usable as new fuel, mirroring MOX) plus a smaller, still-dangerous fission-product waste stream that needs its own long-term disposal path — modeling the real reprocessing tradeoff rather than pretending it's a full reset.
- **Two disposal endpoints matching the two real strategies**: direct burial/deep storage (once-through, simple, permanently "wastes" the U-238/Pu content) versus the reprocessing loop above — giving players the same France-vs-US strategic choice real nuclear programs face.
- **Waste volume kept intentionally small per unit of energy produced**, consistent with the real ~29 mg/kWh figure, so the mechanic doesn't degenerate into a "waste flood" — the interesting problem is duration of danger, not quantity, matching the real-world story.

These ideas assume the parallel decay/radiation-mechanics research defines the actual compressed decay-time model and radiation-exposure mechanics; this document only supplies the physical grounding (isotope mix, real decay-heat curve shape, real storage timescales, real reprocessing behavior) that model should stay consistent with.
